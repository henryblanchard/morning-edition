Fourteen megabytes. Full agentic intelligence.

Needle2 is a single 14-megabyte binary that runs a complete AI agent session in 28MB of RAM. It hits 500 tokens per second on a Raspberry Pi 5 and trades wins with Apple's on-device foundation model at up to seventy times smaller. This is what happens when engineers design for constraint rather than benchmark charts: intelligence compresses further than anyone expected.

Stop softening the machine

A direct, well-argued case against the instinct to make AI output sound more "human": warmer, hedgier, apologetic, personable. The argument is that this impulse is actively harmful. It trains users to trust AI output more than the evidence warrants, and obscures what they are actually reading. Honest, dry, clearly-machine-generated text is the ethical default. Hard to disagree with the conclusion.

The best programming language for coding agents

Dan Luu has quantified token efficiency across programming languages in the context of AI coding agents. The analysis matters: agents that write and read code burn tokens proportional to verbosity. A language requiring twice the tokens to express the same logic costs twice as much per task. The results will rearrange your assumptions about which tools belong in an AI-first stack.

The computer that defied categories

Japan, 1954. Engineers at the University of Tokyo built a computing machine that used neither transistors nor vacuum tubes: the only two technologies anyone believed capable of binary logic. The Parametron exploited magnetic flux resonance instead. It worked, it scaled, and it has just been designated an IEEE Milestone. History keeps finding people who solved the problem differently from everyone who failed at it.

The machine that made a fool of a genius

Mark Twain invested everything he had in the Paige Compositor, a mechanical typesetter of extraordinary ambition and catastrophic unreliability. He believed technological superiority would be enough. It was not. The Linotype overtook it just as his investment reached its peak, and the machine consumed roughly five million dollars in today's terms before it failed. Twain was ruined. The inventor was convinced he was months from victory until he wasn't. The story is a near-perfect parable for any founder who has ever confused the best solution with the winning one.
