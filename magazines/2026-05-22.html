The fungal network that feeds the forest.

On the quiet partnership that keeps most plants alive

Mycorrhizal fungi thread themselves through the roots of the overwhelming majority of land plants, trading scarce minerals and water drawn from the soil in exchange for the sugars a plant makes from sunlight. This essay walks through the biology of that bargain and why it underpins so much of life above ground, from a single garden bed to an entire woodland. The fungi extend a plant's reach far beyond what its own roots could manage, acting as a vast, branching extension of the root system. In return they take a cut of the plant's photosynthetic income, a transaction so old and so widespread that most of the green world simply assumes it. The piece makes a persuasive case that the interesting action in a garden is not the part you can see. Tend the soil and its fungal partners, it argues, and the visible plant tends to look after itself.
