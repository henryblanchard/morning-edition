Eight Decades of Computer Science, Locked Out of the Models

The ACM Digital Library holds 80 years of peer-reviewed computing research: the most structured, rigorously verified body of technical knowledge in existence. Language models cannot read it.

A guest editorial in Communications of the ACM makes the case that this is not merely an access inconvenience. It is a capability ceiling. The gap between what LLMs currently know and what they could reason over is sitting behind a subscription paywall.

The argument for opening the archive is harder to dismiss than it sounds. If we are serious about AI that can do science, we should probably let it read the science. Eighty years of peer review is not a small thing to leave behind a gate.

Peter Norvig Said Ten Years. He Wrote That in 1998. He Is Still Right.

The man who built Google's spell-checker argued in 1998 that serious programming competence requires a decade of deliberate practice, not 21 days and a course certificate. The essay is still online, still correct, and perpetually timely because every generation re-invents the weekend coding bootcamp. In the AI era, the lesson sharpens: the tools accelerate output but not understanding. If you teach people to use AI, teach them what they are actually learning to do.
