Before Reading Your Prompt, Claude Code Sends This Many Tokens

Engineers instrumented both Claude Code and OpenCode, logging every request that passed to Anthropic's endpoint. Teaching Cause and Effect to Machines That Only Know Patterns

Researchers are importing the formal language of causal inference into the study of large language models. The goal: not to describe what a model does, but to establish what it is doing and why, in the same rigorous sense epidemiologists link exposures to outcomes. Whether this approach will succeed is deeply contested. The ambition is worth watching regardless. For the first time, people are seriously attempting to build a science of how LLMs reason, not just a taxonomy of what they get wrong.

The Man Who Invented the Internet Is Handing In His Pass

Vint Cerf co-designed TCP/IP in 1974. His retirement ends an era in which someone who remembered building the original protocols from scratch was still in the room. The institution of his knowledge does not retire with him. A Bug That Lived in Every Linux Machine for Fifteen Years

GhostLock is a use-after-free vulnerability in the Linux kernel's io_uring subsystem: a stack object is freed, its reference is not, and a window opens for an attacker to write arbitrary data into memory. It has been present in every major Linux distribution since io_uring's introduction. Fifteen years. Millions of servers. The researchers describe it without alarm: this is the texture of systems software, where bugs of this severity wait patiently in production until someone looks carefully enough to find them.

When the System Optimises but Nobody Knows Why

Automated systems can now optimise processes without any internal model of what those processes are actually doing. This arxiv paper explores the growing gap between capability and comprehension. The concern is not that automation is wrong, but that automation without mechanistic understanding creates systems that work until they fail, in ways nobody predicted because nobody was required to understand them. For educators building AI courses: this is the argument for teaching students to ask what is happening inside a model, not just what it produces.
