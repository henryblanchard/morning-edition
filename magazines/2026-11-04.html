String Theory, Applied to Robotics

Stringman is an $800 open-source robot that uses cables instead of rigid arms to grip and move objects in room-scale space. Rather than the expensive articulated limbs that define industrial robotics, it suspends a gripper on a network of motorised strings, letting it sweep across a full room with surprising precision.

The build cost is roughly one three-hundredth of a comparable commercial system. The plans are published, the BOM is straightforward, and the team's thesis is that the barrier to embodied robotics is architecture, not materials. The cables make it gentle, too: it handles objects without the rigid-arm problem of a robot that cannot stop itself hitting things.

Why Silicon Valley Is Running on iPhone Chips

Namespace explains how they scale macOS compute to hundreds of machines using Apple Silicon. The economics are counterintuitive at first: why run CI/CD on chips designed for phones? The answer involves performance-per-watt, thermal density, and the peculiarities of Apple's unified memory architecture, which turns out to be excellent for compiling and testing iOS apps at scale.

The performance figures make a surprising argument. The latency numbers for large Xcode builds are hard to match with x86. The piece is also an unusually honest account of the operational complexity that comes with running macOS in a data centre context, Apple's restrictions included.
