Why almost everyone is right-handed.

A new Oxford-led study traces the strong human preference for the right hand to the moment our ancestors started walking upright, rather than to anything in the brain's wiring alone. The argument is that bipedalism freed the dominant hand to specialise while the other steadied posture, a much older origin than previous accounts allowed. A neat reminder that the shape of the body keeps quietly editing the mind, and that the obvious questions in evolutionary biology often turn out to have answers measured in millions of years rather than millimetres of cortex.

OpenAI accepts a watermark, finally.

  what:  SynthID, originally a Google project
  who:   now also signed by OpenAI images
  how:   invisible pixel-level signal
  why:   raises the cost of casual deception
  caveat: a determined forger still strips it

OpenAI has adopted Google's SynthID watermark for AI-generated images and shipped a public verifier alongside it, joining the small but growing list of labs treating provenance as a shipped feature rather than a future white paper. It will not stop a determined forger, but it raises the cost of casual deception, which is most of the problem most days. A line worth teaching: provenance is now a property an image can carry, like EXIF, but harder to strip.

The mistake that gave the eighties their drum sound.

In 1979 the engineer Hugh Padgham mis-routed a talk-back mic during a Peter Gabriel session and accidentally invented the gated-reverb drum sound that ended up defining almost every pop record of the 1980s. The lesson, told here in unusual detail, is that the most influential studio tricks tend to be accidents that someone had the taste to keep. Worth knowing the next time you reach instinctively to undo a happy mistake.
